## Resolved research flags (v1.1, rechecked 2026-11-18)

| ID | Site | v1 concern | What the sources say | Resolution in the script |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-1 | Nan Madol dating | Script used c. 1200–1500 CE, but a 2023 abstract argued for an earlier 11th-century peak. | **Peer-reviewed:** McCoy et al. 2016 (*Quaternary Research* 86:295–303) dates the earliest monumental tomb, built of coral and basalt, to **AD 1180–1200**. UNESCO frames construction within c. 1200–1500 CE. The earlier-peak claim comes from a 2023 EGU **conference abstract**, not a peer-reviewed paper. The first non-monumental islets go back further (c. 900 CE+). | **No change.** "Associated especially with about 1200 to 1500 CE" matches UNESCO, McCoy 2016 and your brief, and doesn't claim an exact start date. Revisit if the 2023 work is published in a journal. |
| R-2 | Puma Punku andesite source | The exact andesite quarry was uncertain (Copacabana Peninsula vs. Ccapia). | Earlier work (Ponce Sanginés et al.) favoured the Copacabana Peninsula. **More recent geochemical sourcing** (Janusek & Williams 2016; follow-up pXRF studies) identifies **Mount Ccapia**, on the far side of Lake Titicaca's southern basin, as the principal andesite source, and the **Kaliri quarry in the Kausani Valley** (~10 km) for the red sandstone. Abandoned andesite blocks at the lake-shore port of Iwawe support transport across the lake. | Narration now says "Andesite came from volcanic sources across the lake" (true under both views). The map in the shot list (row 48) names Kausani and Mount Ccapia. |
| R-3 | Sacsayhuamán largest stone | v1 said "well over a hundred tonnes" with no measured mass behind it. | **No peer-reviewed measured mass exists.** Published estimates for the largest block range from ~100–128 t up to ~200 t, depending on how much of the block is assumed to be buried. Commonly cited height is ~8.5 m (tourism and secondary sources only). | Narration now says "weight estimates ranging from about one hundred to two hundred tonnes". The unsourced height is not used. |
| R-4 | Derinkuyu depth | Your brief said ~60 m, but many sources say ~85 m. | **Official:** Turkey's Ministry of Culture & Tourism museum portal gives an **approximate depth of 85 m** and a ~55 m ventilation shaft/well, and says only about 10% of the complex is open. The district governorship's figure for the eight open floors is ~50 m. So "60 m" is neither the open depth nor the total. | Narration now says "The parts open today go down more than fifty metres, and the full complex is estimated to reach around eighty-five." The shot list adds a depth-bar graphic. Thumbnail #3 is now "85 M DOWN". The existing cutaway prompt is kept (see F-7). **This deliberately departs from the brief's ~60 m figure for accuracy.** |
